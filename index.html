Everyone I've helped with AI started by talking it out.

This year I've helped my son, my wife, and a customer success team of fifty-plus people at work get started with AI. I didn't write anyone's instructions. I set up the conversation – a script of leading questions, drafted with Claude – and they said what they wanted in their own words.

The idea

Most of my AI work starts with me talking.

I do so much more with dictation now. In Granola, in Microsoft 365 Copilot at work, or here in Claude, I open the mic and brain dump, and let the AI pull out the throughline. I think so much better when I'm talking it out, and I think that's true for all of us.

That changes what matters. Getting the prompt perfect isn't as big a deal anymore. Sitting with your thoughts and exploring what you actually want out of AI is. When people get down into the fine details, their instructions get so much better (and so do mine).

So when I help someone, I don't set anything up for them. I set up the conversation: a working model and a script of leading questions, drafted with Claude, that gets them saying what they want in their own words. It's closer to hosting a podcast than writing a manual, and helping people explore what's possible is the part I've gotten really good at. (The same idea, doing the thinking up front, runs through my meal-planning page.)

My son

My son wrote his own honesty rule.

My son is nine, and he uses Gemini through a kids' account I manage (no memory, no custom Gems, chat only). Instead of writing instructions for him, I had Claude draft a short interview script of leading questions, and I recorded him answering it: who the AI should be, how it should talk to him, and what it should do when it's wrong.

He wanted it to talk to him like a third grader, sound like "a nine-year-old boy" who likes Pokémon, and act like "a helpful teacher" that takes its time. When I asked whether it should admit when it doesn't know or answer anyway, he picked admit, and agreed it should ask him to rephrase instead of guessing. The only thing he said it should NEVER do is say it doesn't like Pokémon.

Claude turned the transcript into a 12-bullet prompt he keeps in his notes app. He pastes it in and dictates so he doesn't have to type. On the first run, Gemini misheard him and he started to just answer the next question, so I had him go back and correct it first. He also asked whether it could be his diary. It can't (it doesn't remember anything between chats), so I told him that straight.

The longer goal is teaching him context, constraints, and what he wants back – before he ever has a tool that can act on its own.

The answer shape that worked for him: three options, each with a reason attached, and one recommendation. He said it was good and immediately asked two more questions.
My wife

My wife kept the interview and skipped the structure.

My wife is an accountant, and she was the first person I built this for. We started with a handful of short sessions, each one built around a real task from her week and ordered by how much relief it bought her. The one that mattered most was meeting prep, and its success signal was simple: she walks into the call without opening five different emails first.

Her prompting frame came from how she already works, from source data toward a deliverable: what I have, what I want, and the exception if there is one. That frame is hers. A teacher working from a standard toward a lesson would get a different one.

This summer we set up her personal Claude the same way, starting with an interview script Claude drafted and her answers in her own words. She was specific: answer first and explain after ("a recipe blog in reverse"), act like a tool rather than a friend, and work never goes into personal AI. Then she told me she didn't want a structured intake. She'd rather build her projects up by just talking to it, so that's how she does it.

I see her using AI far more at work now, and at home she uses it for everything.

Packaging it

Once I'd done it twice, I turned it into two Claude skills.

The first helps one person. It writes the interview guide, I record them talking through it, and the transcript turns into four things: a short block for the tool's personalization settings, a longer reference doc in their own vocabulary, a prompting frame of three parts or fewer, and six to eight short sessions sequenced by relief delivered rather than by tool features. A third mode comes back after a few weeks of real use and revises the kit based on how they've actually been using it.

The second does the same job for a group rolling out Microsoft 365 Copilot, Gemini in Google Workspace, or Claude for a team. It starts by writing down who the audience is before any content exists. The Copilot version has been through a real rollout; the Gemini version is still untested.

At work

At work, I'm doing this for fifty-plus people I don't manage.

At Henry Schein One, the ask was a one-page tip sheet for our CS team's Microsoft 365 Copilot rollout. Before building anything, I wrote one sentence about the audience: assume most people have never used AI beyond a quick email. Then I built a session deck, a setup guide, a best practices guide, a starter prompt pack, a full prompt library, and a saved prompt that interviews each CSM and drafts their custom instructions. It marks anything it doesn't know as NEEDS INPUT and never saves on its own. The CSM reviews it and saves it.

Use cases run at 100, 200, and 300 levels, like college course numbers. 100 is finding the tool and making one simple ask, 200 is a real workflow with a specific account or thread, and 300 is pulling across sources and building something repeatable. For prompting I teach AIM (Actor, Input, Mission), a pattern from the community rather than my own, and I teach it honestly: the Actor sets the tone, and Input and Mission carry the quality.

The first session landed heavy. The feedback was that it was a lot, and not practical enough for day-to-day work. What I took from it: everyone with a stake needs to be aligned well before enablement gets planned and structured for an org.

So I changed the format. Instead of another deck, I started a weekly AMA and built a one-page hub to screenshare during it (accordions closed by default, so it stays light until someone needs the detail, and no meeting time on the page, so it never goes stale). People bring a real question, and the page routes it to where it should go.

It's early. I don't manage anyone on that team, and I built the AMA before lining up the people who do. It's the honest gap in how I work: I can design the system, and getting a team I have no authority over to pick it up is the part I'm still learning.

There are no adoption numbers on this page because there aren't any yet. When there are, they'll go here.

What's still open.

Next up is a teacher friend who asked me to help him get set up. We haven't found the hour yet – he's stretched thin, which is exactly why he asked. The interview guide is ready when he is.

One person at a time, this works because they trust me and the setup is built around their week. With fifty people, neither of those comes for free. How much of the method carries over, and what has to come from the people who do have the authority, is what I'm working out now.
